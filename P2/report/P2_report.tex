\section{Exercise 1}

\subsection{Question}
Simulation of a simple Lennard-Jones fluid, such as liquid argon, with a hand-made FORTRAN MD code which incorporates the Leap-frog Verlet algorithm.

\paragraph{STEP 1: Understanding the code.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Open the FORTRAN file \texttt{leapfroglj.f} and have a look.
  \item Note that you'll need 2 auxiliary files: (1)~\texttt{leap-lj.data} and (2)~\texttt{leap-conf.data}, containing (1)~data input and (2)~initial coordinates and velocities, respectively.
  \item The main program is ready to simulate a LJ liquid of 500 atoms of mass $39.948\text{ uma}$, $\sigma = 3.405\text{ \AA}$ and $\varepsilon/k_B = 119.8\text{ K}$ (Argon), with a time step of $\Delta t = 0.01\text{ ps}$. Remember that the LJ potential is of the form:
\begin{equation}
  V_{\text{LJ}}(r) = 4\varepsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12} - \left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right].
\end{equation}
  \item Compile the code and make a run of $10000$ time steps. If you compute the temperature $T$ and total energy $E$ (kinetic + potential) you will get the following figures.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Code}
For this step we ran the supplied leap-frog program and added the accumulation and printing of the averages. The run uses the parameters of the statement ($10000$ steps, $N = 500$). The full program, with every later addition, is listed in the Annex.

With $L^* = 8.427$ ($\rho^* = 0.835$) and $\Delta t^* = 4.637\times 10^{-3}$ ($\tau = 2.156\text{ ps}$), the $10000$ steps cover $t^* = 46.37$ ($100\text{ ps}$). Figure~\ref{fig:ex1} shows $T$ and $E_{\text{tot}} = E_{\text{kin}} + E_{\text{pot}}$.

\subsection{Discussion \& Notes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Energy:} leap-frog Verlet is symplectic, so $E_{\text{tot}}$ should only oscillate. We get $\langle E_{\text{tot}}\rangle = -2266.65$, and $\sigma_E = 0.537$ ($0.024\%$), a very small fluctuation.
  \item \textbf{Temperature:} $\langle T^*\rangle = 0.713$ ($85.4\text{ K}$), $\sigma_T = 0.0175$ ($2.5\%$). That is a much larger relative fluctuation than for $E_{\text{tot}}$, as $E_{\text{kin}}$ and $E_{\text{pot}}$ are exchanged. Both curves match the reference figures for a dense liquid.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Results}
For this exercise we reused the same $10000$-step trajectory as in Exercise~1 ($N = 500$, $\rho^* = 0.835$, $\langle T^*\rangle = 0.713$, $r_c = 2.5$). The radial distribution function was built by histogramming the pair distances into $1000$ shells up to $L^*/2 = 4.21$, and each shell was then normalised by the number of atoms an ideal gas of the same density would place in it. The pressure was split into a kinetic and a virial part, $P = P_{\text{kin}} + P_{\text{vir}} + P_{\text{tail}}$, with $P_{\text{kin}} = 2E_{\text{kin}}/(3V)$ and $P_{\text{vir}} = \frac{1}{3V}\sum_{i<j}\vec{r}_{ij}\cdot\vec{f}_{ij}$. Since the potential is cut at $r_c$, the interactions beyond the cutoff are recovered with the usual tail corrections, which assume the fluid is uniform ($g(r) = 1$) past $r_c$:
\begin{equation}
  E_{\text{tail}} = \frac{8\pi}{3}\rho N\left(\frac{1}{3r_c^9} - \frac{1}{r_c^3}\right) = -223.67, \qquad
  P_{\text{tail}} = \frac{16\pi}{3}\rho^2\left(\frac{2}{3r_c^9} - \frac{1}{r_c^3}\right) = -0.7465.
\end{equation}
Both depend only on the density, so they are constants added to the averages (Fig.~\ref{fig:ex2}, Table~\ref{tab:ex2}).

\subsection{Discussion \& Notes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Results vs.\ expectations:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{RDF:} As expected, $g(r)$ is zero inside the repulsive core ($r \lesssim 0.93$), since two atoms cannot get that close. It then rises to a sharp first peak of $g = 3.00$ at $r = 1.089$, the shell of nearest neighbours near the LJ minimum ($2^{1/6} \approx 1.12$), drops to a minimum of $g = 0.576$ at $r = 1.544$, and shows two weaker peaks at $r = 2.079$ ($g = 1.28$) and $r = 2.985$ ($g = 1.11$), the second and third shells. By $r = L/2$ the oscillations have died out ($g = 0.993 \approx 1$): each atom has well-defined neighbours but, unlike in a crystal, this order is lost after a few atomic diameters.

    \item \emph{Energy:} The mean total energy is $\langle E_{\text{tot}}\rangle/N = -4.98$ per particle, negative as it should be for a bound liquid. The tail accounts for $9\%$ of it: although $V(r_c) \approx -0.016$ is very small, there are many neighbours beyond $r_c$.

    \item \emph{Pressure:} The kinetic part, $\langle P_{\text{kin}}\rangle = 0.5945$, agrees with the ideal-gas value $\rho^* \langle T^*\rangle = 0.596$, as it must. The virial inside the cutoff is small and positive ($0.100$), as the repulsive core only slightly outweighs the attraction, while the tail is purely attractive and large ($-0.746$). Together they almost cancel the kinetic term, leaving $P_{\text{tot}} = -0.052$, very close to zero. Without the tail we would have obtained $P^* \approx +0.69$, so it is essential for the pressure, much more than for the energy.

    \item \emph{Convergence:} The two totals converge at very different rates. $\langle E_{\text{tot}}\rangle$ barely moves, changing by only $0.2$ ($<0.01\%$) once a few thousand steps are averaged. $\langle P_{\text{tot}}\rangle$ is a small difference of large terms, so any fluctuation of $P_{\text{vir}}$ shows up directly in it; it only settles after about $5000$ steps ($t^* \approx 23$), staying within $\pm 0.006$ afterwards.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Conclusion:}
  The shell structure of $g(r)$, the negative energy and the near-zero pressure consistently describe a dense Lennard-Jones liquid. The energy converges quickly, while the pressure needs the tail correction and at least about $5000$ steps.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Question}
\paragraph{STEP 3: Analysing the dynamics.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Modify the code to calculate the velocity autocorrelation function (VACF) and the mean square displacement (MSD):
\begin{equation}
  C(t) = \bigl\langle \vec{v}(t)\cdot\vec{v}(0)\bigr\rangle,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  r^{2}(t) = \bigl\langle \bigl[\vec{r}(t)-\vec{r}(0)\bigr]^{2}\bigr\rangle.
\end{equation}
  Compute the VACF and the MSD for a run of $10000$ steps. Plot the results.
  \item Write a code to calculate the spectral density (SDW) of the VACF:
\begin{equation}
  S(\omega) = \int_0^{\infty} dt\, C(t)\cos(\omega t).
  \label{eq:sdw}
\end{equation}
  Use a Simpson $3/8$ rule or similar. Plot the results.
  \item Use Einstein's diffusion formula to obtain the self-diffusion coefficient $D$:
\begin{equation}
  D = \lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{6t}\, r^{2}(t).
  \label{eq:einstein}
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discussion \& Notes}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Results vs.\ expectations:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{VACF:} The initial value $C(0) = 2.138$ agrees with the equipartition theorem, which gives a mean-square velocity of $\langle v^2\rangle = 3\langle T^*\rangle = 2.139$ (for $m = 1$). The velocity autocorrelation function $C(t)$ decays rapidly thereafter, retaining only $21\%$ of its initial value by $t^* = 0.1$. It crosses zero at $t^* = 0.139$ ($0.30\text{ ps}$) and reaches a minimum of $C = -0.256$ at $t^* = 0.195$ ($0.42\text{ ps}$). This negative correlation indicates backscattering: on average, a particle reverses its velocity after colliding with the transient ``cage'' formed by its nearest neighbours (corresponding to the first radial distribution peak at $r = 1.09$ in Exercise~2). Such rebound behaviour is characteristic of dense liquids, in contrast to dilute gases where $C(t)$ decays monotonically toward zero. By $t^* \approx 0.64$, $|C| < 0.02$: the atom has lost memory of its initial velocity.

    \item \emph{MSD:} At very short timescales ($t^* \lesssim 0.05$), atomic motion is purely ballistic, satisfying $\langle \Delta r^2(t)\rangle \approx \langle v^2\rangle t^2$. Beyond this regime, frequent collisions deflect the particle, initiating a crossover into the diffusive regime; for $t^* \gtrsim 1$, $\langle \Delta r^2(t)\rangle$ scales linearly with time, as expected for normal (Fickian) diffusion.

    \item \emph{Direct Einstein ratio:} Calculating the instantaneous ratio $\langle \Delta r^2(t)\rangle / (6t)$ from~\eqref{eq:einstein} yields $0.0372$ at $t^* = 1$ and $0.0340$ at $t^* = 4.98$. Although this quotient asymptotically approaches the true diffusion coefficient, it systematically overestimates $D$ at finite times because it does not decouple the early-time ballistic displacement. A linear fit over the diffusive regime ($t^* \gtrsim 1$) avoids this: the slope gives $D = 0.0331$, and the non-zero intercept absorbs the ballistic offset.

    \item \emph{Spectral density $S(\omega)$:} The velocity power spectrum has its maximum at $\omega = 8$ ($S = 0.158$) and decays sharply at high frequencies ($S = 5.4 \times 10^{-3}$ at $\omega = 40$; $5 \times 10^{-5}$ at $\omega = 100$). The value at the origin, $S(0) = \int_0^\infty C(t)\,dt = 0.100$, is non-zero because the atoms diffuse, and the Green--Kubo formula relates it directly to the self-diffusion coefficient:
    \begin{equation}
      D = \frac{1}{3}\int_0^\infty C(t)\,dt = \frac{S(0)}{3} = 0.0334 \approx 0.0331,
    \end{equation}
    within $1\%$ of the MSD linear fit.
  \end{itemize}

  \item \textbf{Conclusion:}
  The minimum of $C(t)$ (the rebound against the first shell of neighbours seen in $g(r)$), the peak of $S(\omega)$ at $\omega = 8$ and a single diffusion coefficient from two independent routes, $D = 0.0331$ from the MSD and $0.0334$ from Green--Kubo, describe the dynamics of a dense Lennard-Jones liquid.
\end{itemize}
